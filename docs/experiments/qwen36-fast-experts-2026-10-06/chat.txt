User: Hi! I'm planning a three-day trip to Rome in late October. I've never been there. Where should I start?

Assistant: Rome in late October is a great choice: the summer crowds are thinner, the weather is usually mild (around 15 to 22 °C), and the evening light on the old buildings is beautiful. For three days, a good plan is to give each day one area of the city, so you walk more and travel less.

Day one: ancient Rome. Start early at the Colosseum, then walk to the Roman Forum and the Palatine Hill, which share one ticket. In the afternoon, go up the Capitoline Hill for the view over the Forum, then down to Piazza Venezia.

Day two: the Vatican. Book the Vatican Museums for the morning, as early as you can, and leave at least three hours for them; the Sistine Chapel is at the end of the visit. Then walk to St. Peter's Basilica. In the evening, cross the river to the area around Piazza Navona.

Day three: the historic center. The Pantheon, the Trevi Fountain, the Spanish Steps and Campo de' Fiori are all within walking distance of each other. In the evening, Trastevere is the classic place for dinner.

Would you like suggestions for food, or tips on tickets and transport?

User: Both, please! And is it worth getting a Roma Pass?

Assistant: Let's start with the Roma Pass. It comes in two versions: 48 hours and 72 hours. The 72-hour pass includes free entry to the first museum or archaeological site you visit, reduced prices for the following ones, and unlimited public transport for three days. Whether it is worth it depends on your plan. With the itinerary above, the Colosseum ticket alone covers a good part of the price, and you will probably use the metro and buses a few times. However, the Vatican Museums are not included, because the Vatican is a separate state. If you prefer walking, which in the center is often faster than the bus, a simple Colosseum ticket and a few single transport tickets may cost less.

For tickets, book the Colosseum and the Vatican Museums online a few weeks in advance. Time slots sell out, especially on weekends. For the Colosseum, the standard ticket includes the Forum and the Palatine; there are also tickets with access to the underground and the arena floor, which are limited and sell out first.

For transport, Rome has only three metro lines, but they reach the main sights: line A stops near the Vatican (Ottaviano), the Spanish Steps (Spagna) and Termini station; line B stops at the Colosseum. A single ticket is valid for 100 minutes on buses and one metro ride. You can also pay with a contactless card directly at the turnstiles.

Now food, which is half the reason to go to Rome! Try the four classic pasta dishes: carbonara (eggs, pecorino, guanciale and black pepper, never cream), cacio e pepe, amatriciana and gricia. For a quick lunch, pizza al taglio, sold by weight, is perfect between two sights. Supplì, fried rice balls with tomato and mozzarella, are a great snack. In late October you may also find the first artichokes, although the real season starts a bit later.

A few practical tips: avoid restaurants with menus in many languages and photos right next to the big monuments; walk two or three streets away and prices drop while quality goes up. Coffee at the bar counter costs much less than at a table. And always carry a small bottle: the public fountains, called nasoni, give fresh drinking water all over the city.

User: That's really helpful. One more thing: I'm a bit worried about pickpockets. Any advice?

Assistant: It's a sensible thing to think about, and a few simple habits make a big difference. Pickpockets in Rome work mostly where people are distracted and close together: on crowded buses, especially line 64 to the Vatican and line 40, in the metro at rush hour, and in the crowds around the Trevi Fountain and the Spanish Steps.

Keep your phone and wallet in a front pocket or in a bag that closes with a zip, worn in front of you in crowded places. Don't hang your bag on the back of a chair at a café table. Be careful with people who approach you with a petition to sign, a bracelet to tie on your wrist or a map to show you: it is often a way to distract you while someone else reaches for your pockets.

If you take a taxi, use only the official white taxis from the taxi ranks, or call one through an app. At the airport, ignore drivers who offer a ride inside the arrivals hall.

That said, Rome is generally a safe city for visitors, and with these precautions you can relax and enjoy it. Is there anything else you'd like to plan, like a day trip outside the city?

User: Actually yes. If I had a fourth day, what would you recommend outside Rome?

Assistant: With a fourth day, you have some lovely options, all reachable by train in about an hour or less.

Ostia Antica is the closest and, for many visitors, the best surprise: it was the port of ancient Rome, and its ruins are large, well preserved and much less crowded than the Forum. You can walk along the old streets, see the theater, the baths and the mosaics of the shipping offices. The train leaves from the Piramide station, next to the metro stop of the same name, and takes about thirty minutes.

Tivoli is another classic choice. It has two gardens of very different kinds: Villa d'Este, with its Renaissance fountains, and Hadrian's Villa, the enormous country residence of the emperor Hadrian. Seeing both in one day is possible, but you will need to move between them by bus or taxi, because they are a few kilometers apart.

If you prefer a town with a different atmosphere, Orvieto, in Umbria, is about an hour and a quarter by train. It sits on top of a cliff of volcanic rock, and its cathedral has one of the most striking facades in Italy. The town is small enough to explore on foot, and the local white wine is famous.

My suggestion would depend on what you enjoyed most in Rome: if the ancient sites fascinated you, go to Ostia Antica; if you'd like gardens and villas, Tivoli; and if you want to see a different side of Italy, Orvieto.

User: I think Ostia Antica sounds perfect. How much time should I plan there, and is there anything to know before going?

Assistant: Ostia Antica is a great pick. Plan about three to four hours for the site itself, plus the train ride, so it fits comfortably into a morning or an afternoon, and you will still have time for a relaxed lunch.

Before going, there are a few things worth knowing. First, check the opening days: like many archaeological sites in Italy, Ostia Antica is usually closed on Mondays. Second, the site is large and mostly in the open, with little shade, so bring water, a hat and comfortable shoes, because many of the ancient streets are paved with uneven stones. Third, there is a small museum inside the site with statues and objects found during the excavations; it is often overlooked, but it is worth half an hour.
